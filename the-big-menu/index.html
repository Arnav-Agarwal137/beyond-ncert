Day 0 for every idea: the salvage hunt

Free parts are hiding in the house. Broken toy cars are the jackpot: motors, gearboxes, wheels, sometimes a working remote-control board. Old CD/DVD drives give small motors (the tray motor), gears and a sliding rail. Dead printers give gears, steel rods and belts. Also check battery fans, electric toothbrushes, phone vibration motors, and an old cordless drill (strong motor + gearbox + battery). Old transformers and relays contain thin copper wire you can unwind.

Safety, non-negotiable: battery power only, never the wall socket. Avoid loose lithium cells pulled from laptop packs; use AA cells. Hot glue burns, blades cut — the boring stuff.

A. No electricity at all

Cardboard, sticks, string, marbles, water. The "brain" is a shape you cut or a lever you place.

Easy

1Copying drawing arm ₹0

DoesYou draw a small shape; the arm draws the same shape twice as big beside it. (A pantograph.)

You control it byYour hand.

Made from4 ice-cream sticks, 4 pins, a pencil, tape.

Tricky bitThe four sticks must form a perfect stretchy rectangle (a parallelogram) — pins loose enough to swing, tight enough not to wobble.

Afterwards you can explainHow linkages copy and scale motion — the trick inside drafting machines and engraving tools.

2One-finger crane ₹0

DoesA hand-cranked crane that lifts a brick or a full water bottle with one finger's effort, because you built a chain of small-wheel-drives-big-wheel steps (a gear or pulley reduction).

You control it byA crank.

Made fromCardboard, skewers, bottle caps as pulleys with rubber bands (or cardboard gears with toothpick teeth), string.

Tricky bitWheels that don't slip or jam. And feeling the trade in your hand: five turns of the crank for one turn of the drum — easy but slow.

Afterwards you can explainMechanical advantage and gear ratios — why a bicycle has gears.

3Grabber claw on a stick ₹0

DoesSqueeze a handle; a claw a metre away closes and picks up a sock (a linkage gripper, like a shopkeeper's reach-extender).

You control it byA squeeze trigger.

Made fromA long cardboard tube or stick, string or stiff wire, ice-cream-stick fingers, a rubber band to open.

Tricky bitMaking the fingers close together, not one at a time.

Afterwards you can explainLevers, and how pulling one thing can move several things.

4Robot hand that copies yours ₹0

DoesA cardboard-and-straw hand with strings inside each finger, like tendons. Tie the other ends to a glove on your hand: curl your fingers and it curls its fingers.

You control it byWearing the glove.

Made fromCardboard, drinking straws cut into finger segments, string, an old glove, rubber bands to straighten.

Tricky bitString routing — every corner adds friction. The thumb is the boss fight: it must swing sideways to meet the fingers.

Afterwards you can explainHow your own hand works (the muscles are in your forearm; tendons pull the fingers) and what "teleoperation" means.

Easy–Medium

5Aiming catapult with a dial ₹0

DoesYou say "hit the cup at 2 metres." You check your own chart, set the dial (angle or pull-back), fire, and hit it. The machine is simple; the engineering is the chart you build by testing.

You control it byNaming the target and dialling it in.

Made fromIce-cream sticks, rubber bands, a spoon, a cardboard base with a marked scale.

Tricky bitConsistency — if it throws differently every time, no chart can save you. Curing the wobble is the lesson.

Afterwards you can explainCalibration (turning a rough machine into a precise one by measuring) and how thrown things fly.

Medium

6Cam-programmed car ₹0

DoesA car with no remote and no electronics that drives a route you designed in advance — an S, a square, a figure-8. A bumpy disc turning with the wheels (a cam) nudges the steering as it goes round; the bumps are the program.

You control it byCutting a new disc for a new route.

Made fromCardboard, skewers, straws, bottle-cap wheels, a rubber band or a salvaged toy motor.

Tricky bitThe disc must turn slowly (one turn = the whole route), so you need a slow-down step between the wheels and the disc. Also floor grip.

Afterwards you can explainCams — how a shape can be a set of instructions, the way old washing-machine timers and music boxes were programmed.

7Cam automaton ₹0

DoesTurn a crank; a cardboard figure hammers, waves, nods — or a pen draws a repeating pattern — in an order you decided by shaping the discs.

You control it byDesigning the bumps.

Made fromCardboard, skewers, a shoebox.

Tricky bitGetting two or three motions to happen at the right moments relative to each other (timing).

Afterwards you can explainHow rotation becomes up-and-down motion, and the same "shape = program" idea as #6.

Hard

8Walking machine ₹0–150

DoesA four-legged machine that walks with legs, not wheels, when you crank it or attach a toy motor. Each leg is a set of connected sticks (a Klann linkage) that traces a foot-step shape.

You control it byA crank or a switch.

Made fromStiff cardboard or ice-cream sticks, pins/paper clips as joints, straws as sleeves, coat-hanger wire for the crank, hot glue.

Tricky bitPrecision — change one stick by 2 mm and the foot drags. Cardboard bends; joints must be free but firm.

Afterwards you can explainLinkages, why proportions matter, and how one spinning shaft becomes a walking motion.

Easy–Medium

9Logic lock (the AND/OR box) ₹0

DoesA cardboard box that opens only when lever A AND lever B are both pulled — or, built differently, when A OR B is pulled — or only when a lever is NOT pulled. You're building the three basic decision-makers (logic gates) out of levers.

You control it byPulling the right levers.

Made fromCardboard, skewers, rubber bands.

Tricky bitDesigning the AND: two levers that together move a bar far enough, but neither alone. Then chaining gates: (A AND B) OR C.

Afterwards you can explainLogic gates — the atoms every computer is made of — and you'll have touched them.

Medium

10Combination lock ₹0

DoesThree cardboard discs on a shaft, each with one notch. Only when you dial the right code do all three notches line up under a bar; the bar drops in and the latch opens.

You control it byA code you chose.

Made fromCardboard discs, a skewer, a paper-clip bar, a box.

Tricky bitThe bar must fall only when all notches align — sloppy discs open for nearby codes too. That's tolerance.

Afterwards you can explainHow a real combination padlock works, and why precision equals security.

Hard

11Coin-counting candy machine ₹0

DoesDrop in a coin: a slot sized to the right coin lets it pass and drops wrong sizes into a reject tray. Each right coin pushes a lever that turns a toothed wheel one notch (a ratchet — a wheel that only turns one way). On the third notch, a pin releases the candy gate. Then it resets.

You control it byPaying three coins.

Made fromCardboard, skewers, rubber bands, paper clips.

Tricky bitThree mechanisms that must all work — sorting by size, counting reliably, releasing once. Each is fine alone; together they fight.

Afterwards you can explainRatchets, counting mechanisms, sorting by geometry — the guts of vending machines and turnstiles.

12Marble adding machine ₹0–200

DoesLittle seesaws on a tilted board. A marble hitting a seesaw flips it: pointing left = 0, right = 1. Load two numbers as rows of marbles, pull a lever, and the seesaws end up showing the sum in binary. When a marble hits a seesaw already at 1, it flips back to 0 and rolls on to the next — that rolling-on is the carry, exactly like 1 + 1 = 10 in binary. Ask it 5 + 7; it shows 12.

You control it byLoading numbers as marbles.

Made fromFoamboard, stiff cardboard or thin plywood, identical marbles, pins as pivots.

Tricky bitOne seesaw must flip 50 times out of 50 before you build the second. Friction and slightly different marble sizes will humble you.

Afterwards you can explainBinary numbers and how a computer physically adds. Rare knowledge.

Easy–Medium

13Self-filling water bowl ₹0–50

DoesLike the tank behind a toilet: a bottle of water sits above a bowl; a floating cap on a lever squashes a soft tube shut when the water is high enough, and lets go when it drops. The bowl stays at the level you set — forever, with no electricity. Keeps a plant tray or a pet bowl topped up.

You control it bySetting where the float sits.

Made fromA bottle, soft aquarium tubing (₹20), a bottle cap as the float, a lever, a bowl.

Tricky bitThe lever must squash the tube fully shut with only a float's push. Leverage!

Afterwards you can explainFeedback control — the same idea as a thermostat and cruise control, done with a floating cap. The simplest real "controller" you can build.

B. Air and water

Syringes, tubes and balloons. Pushing with water gives force; pushing with air gives springiness.

Medium

14Hydraulic arm, the physics version ~₹150

DoesAn arm with a turning base, shoulder, elbow and gripper, each moved by a pair of syringes joined by a tube of water. Push the small syringe; water pushes the big one — with more force but less travel. The challenge: choose syringe sizes on purpose (measure, predict, then test) so one finger's push lifts a 1 kg bottle.

You control it byYour hands, remotely.

Made from8 syringes in mixed sizes (pharmacy, ₹5–15 each), 3 m tubing, cardboard or ice-cream sticks, pins, rubber bands, glue.

Tricky bitAir bubbles make joints spongy; leaks; and your first shoulder collapses under its own weight until you move its syringe farther from the joint (leverage again).

Afterwards you can explainHow hydraulics multiply force (the JCB principle), and torque.

15Soft robot gripper ~₹50

DoesFingers made from long balloons (or the fingers of a rubber glove) with tape along one side. Push air in from a syringe and they curl, because the taped side can't stretch. Three of them around a ring make a hand that lifts an egg without breaking it. This is what soft-robotics labs build.

You control it byA syringe of air.

Made fromModelling balloons or a rubber glove, tape, straws, a syringe, rubber bands to seal.

Tricky bitSealing. Air escapes from everywhere.

Afterwards you can explainWhy air is springy and water isn't (pneumatic vs. hydraulic), and how a soft thing can grip a delicate thing.

Medium

16Hovercraft ₹0–100

DoesA cardboard or foam disc with a plastic-bag skirt underneath and a fan blowing air into it. It floats a few millimetres off the floor and glides at a touch. Big enough to carry a book.

You control it byA fan switch (steering is the upgrade: a second fan for push).

Made fromCardboard or foam board, a plastic bag, tape, a salvaged battery fan or a computer fan (needs 8 AA cells in a row to make 12 V).

Tricky bitThe skirt — air must leak out evenly all round or it tips and scrapes.

Afterwards you can explainAir cushions, and why pressure × area = lift.

C. Electricity — but you build the brain

No Arduino, no code. The brain is a switch you make, a coil you wind, or a ₹10 chip you wire by hand.

Easy

17Wired joystick rover ~₹250

DoesA car on a wire tether. Two switches you make yourself from paper clips and thumbtacks; each flips one motor forward or backward. Both forward = straight; opposite = spins on the spot (tank steering).

You control it byYour two homemade switches.

Made from2 small geared motors + wheels (salvaged or ~₹150), a 4×AA holder, wire, paper clips, thumbtacks, cardboard.

Tricky bitMaking a switch that reverses a motor (you swap which wire touches plus and minus). Once you get it, it's a lightbulb moment.

Afterwards you can explainCircuits, and why swapping the wires reverses a motor. Honestly the simplest electric project here — a good step before 21–24.

Easy–Medium

18Electric buzzer you build ~₹150

DoesA coil of wire around a nail (an electromagnet) next to a springy strip of tin. Press the button: the coil pulls the strip, which breaks its own connection, so the coil lets go, so the strip springs back and reconnects, so the coil pulls again — fifty times a second. It buzzes on its own. That's how doorbells worked for a century.

You control it byA button.

Made fromThin enamelled copper wire (~₹150 a spool, or unwind an old transformer), a nail, tin from a can, a wood or cardboard base, a battery.

Tricky bitThe gap — too big and it never pulls, too small and it sticks. And sanding the coating off the wire ends (everyone forgets).

Afterwards you can explainElectromagnets, and how a machine can switch itself on and off — the first "automatic" machine.

19Electromagnet crane ~₹150

DoesYour one-finger crane from #2, but the hook is a nail wrapped in wire. Press the button: it grabs pins and paper clips. Release: it drops them. Sort a mixed pile into a tray.

You control it byA button, and the crank.

Made fromThe crane, a nail, magnet wire, a battery, a switch.

Tricky bitMaking it strong enough (hundreds of turns of wire) without draining the battery in a minute.

Afterwards you can explainElectricity makes magnetism, and why more turns means stronger.

20Telegraph → homemade relay → remote motor ~₹200

DoesFirst, a Morse key and a clicker (an electromagnet snapping a tin strip): talk between rooms in dots and dashes. Then the twist: let the electromagnet press a second switch that turns on a motor's separate, stronger circuit. You've built a relay — a switch that electricity presses instead of your finger — and controlled a motor in another room over one thin wire.

You control it byTapping Morse.

Made fromMagnet wire, nails, tin, wood or cardboard, battery, thin wire, a salvaged motor.

Tricky bitA coil strong enough to snap the switch reliably; a spring to return it.

Afterwards you can explainHow a weak signal controls a strong machine — the core of all control, and the ancestor of the transistor.

Medium

21Line follower ~₹250

DoesTwo "eyes" (light sensors, LDRs) point at the floor, one each side, each with a small light. Over white floor an eye sees bright and switches its motor on; over black tape it sees dark and switches its motor off — so the car turns back toward the line. It follows any tape path you lay: loops, S-curves, a figure-8.

You control it byLaying tape.

Made from2 motors + wheels, 2 LDRs (₹10 each), 2 white LEDs, 2 transistors that can handle a motor (TIP122, ₹15 each), a few resistors, a knob resistor (potentiometer) for sensitivity, 2 diodes, battery holder, cardboard, black tape.

Tricky bitSunlight — it works at night and fails at breakfast until you shield the eyes.

Afterwards you can explainThe transistor as an electric switch, and the basic loop of all robotics: sense → decide → move → repeat.

22Torch-steered robot ~₹250

DoesSame parts as #21, but the eyes point forward and the wires are crossed: left eye drives right motor. Shine a torch and it drives toward the beam. Uncross the wires and it runs away from light instead. Behaviour from wiring alone — no computer.

You control it byA torch beam.

Made fromSame as #21, plus two straws so each eye only sees straight ahead.

Tricky bitBalancing the two sides so it drives straight when both eyes see the same light.

Afterwards you can explainHow simple wiring can look like intelligence (Braitenberg vehicles). Build #21, convert to #22 in an afternoon.

23Back-and-forth string shuttle ~₹250

DoesA little motorised carriage that rides a string stretched between two rooms. A bump switch at each end flips it into reverse automatically. It shuttles notes, snacks, or a bell that rings on arrival. Add a "hold" so it waits until you send it.

You control it bySending it.

Made from1 motor + a pulley on the string, 2 small switches (₹20 each), a switch that stays flipped (a DPDT relay, ₹40) wired to remember its direction, battery, cardboard.

Tricky bitWiring the relay so it remembers which way it was going after the bump switch is released.

Afterwards you can explainEnd-stops and latching — the logic inside every elevator, printer head and automatic gate.

Hard

24Bumper-reflex robot ~₹300

DoesDrives forward. A whisker touches the wall → it backs up and turns for about a second → carries on. Explores a room by itself. The "about a second" comes from a part that fills with charge like a tiny bucket (a capacitor) through a narrow pipe (a resistor): an egg-timer made of two ₹5 parts.

You control it byWhere you put the furniture — it drives itself.

Made from2 motors, 2 small switches or homemade wire whiskers, a DPDT relay, a big capacitor, a transistor, resistors, battery, cardboard.

Tricky bitPredicting the timer on paper, then finding out whether physics agrees; whiskers that reset.

Afterwards you can explainRelays, timing with capacitors, and reflexes without a brain.

25Clap-controlled machine ~₹150

DoesClap: a fan, motor or car starts. Clap again: it stops. A microphone hears the clap, a ₹10 chip (the 555) turns the messy sound into one clean click, another chip remembers on/off and drives the motor.

You control it byClapping.

Made fromAn electret mic (₹15), 2 small transistors, a 555 timer, a CD4017 chip, a relay or power transistor, capacitors, resistors, a motor, battery.

Tricky bitThe motor's own noise re-triggers the mic. You'll fight it (move the mic, add a capacitor) and win.

Afterwards you can explainAmplifying tiny signals, timing, and how a circuit can remember — the seed of computer memory.

Very hard

26Copycat knob (a servo from scratch) ~₹200

DoesTwo knobs (potentiometers). Turn knob A by hand; a motor turns knob B until it matches. A "which is bigger?" chip (an op-amp, ₹15) compares the two and drives the motor toward agreement. This is exactly what every robot joint does.

You control it byTurning a knob.

Made from2 potentiometers (₹20 each), 1 LM358 op-amp, 4 transistors or a relay, a motor, battery.

Tricky bitIt overshoots and hunts back and forth around the target. Understanding why — and calming it — is the beginning of control engineering.

Afterwards you can explainFeedback control, the thing that makes a machine follow a command instead of just running. Very hard for a beginner, but the single most valuable idea on this list.

Warm-ups: 30–60 minutes each, for a Day-1 win

  • Bristlebot. A toothbrush head + the tiny vibrating motor from an old phone or toy + a coin cell. It skitters. Shift the weight and it curves — your first "tuning." ▶ Videos
  • Wind your own motor. 30 turns of thin wire, a fridge magnet, two safety pins, one AA cell. It spins. Settles forever what "motor" means. ▶ Videos
  • Nail electromagnet. Wire around a nail + a battery lifts paper clips. Count turns vs. clips lifted and graph it. Feeds ideas 18, 19 and 20. ▶ Videos
  • Pencil speed dial. A thick 6B pencil stripe on paper is a resistor. Slide a wire along it and a small motor speeds up and slows down. ▶ Videos

Plain-words glossary

Motor
Turns electricity into spinning.
Battery
Pushes electricity round a loop; the push is called voltage.
Circuit
The loop. Break it anywhere and everything stops.
Switch
A gap you can close.
Resistor
A narrow pipe for electricity; slows the flow.
Potentiometer
A knob-shaped adjustable resistor.
Capacitor
A tiny bucket that stores charge and empties it later; the heart of simple timers.
Diode
A one-way valve for electricity; protects your parts from a motor's kick.
LDR
A resistor that changes with light — an "eye."
Transistor
A switch that a tiny current presses. Millions of these make a computer chip.
Relay
A switch that an electromagnet presses. The transistor's grandfather.
555 timer
A ₹10 chip that makes clean pulses and blinks; the most-built chip ever.
Op-amp
A chip that compares two voltages and says which is bigger.
Linkage
Sticks joined by pins so moving one moves the others in a designed way.
Cam
A bumpy wheel that pushes a follower as it turns; shape = instructions.
Gear ratio
How many turns in for one turn out; trades speed for force.
Hydraulic / pneumatic
Pushing with water / pushing with air.
Feedback
The machine checks the result and corrects itself. Float valve, thermostat, servo — same idea.
