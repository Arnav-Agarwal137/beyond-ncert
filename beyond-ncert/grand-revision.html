Day 1 · Block 1

The Language of Change

~40 min · Derivatives → partials → gradients → optimization → approximation → logs. This is the vocabulary every later block speaks in.

1.1MathematicsW1 · D1What a Derivative Really MeansZoom into any curve until it looks straight — the slope of that straight piece is the derivative.

Forget d/dx(xⁿ)=nxⁿ⁻¹ for a moment. The derivative answers one question: if I nudge the input a tiny bit, how much does the output move? Geometrically, it's the slope of the tangent line at a point. Newton wrote it f′(x), Leibniz wrote it dy/dx — same idea, two notations.

The most misunderstood case: a derivative of zero doesn't mean nothing is happening. It means the rate of change is momentarily zero — which is exactly what happens at the top of a peak or the bottom of a valley. That single fact powers optimization (this block) and every "system settles here" claim later.

Why it matters: every rate in this plan — drug clearance, enzyme speed, population growth — is this one picture wearing a different costume. This was the load-bearing day of the whole plan.
1.2MathematicsW1 · D2Partial Derivatives & Multivariable FunctionsHold every other variable still, and ask how the output changes with just one.

Most of reality depends on more than one input: a receptor's response depends on ligand concentration, receptor density, and time; a drug's effect depends on dose, body weight, and kidney function together. A partial derivative (written with the curly ∂, as in ∂f/∂x) asks: freeze everything else, vary one input, watch the output.

That's it — the whole day existed to stop you flinching at the ∂ symbol. A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative taken while pretending the other variables are constants.

Why it matters: a gradient (Block 1, below) is nothing but a bundle of these — one per direction. Biology is multivariable by default; this is the first formal tool for "more than one thing changing at once."
1.3MathematicsW3 · D1Gradient VectorsBundle the partial derivatives into one arrow — it points in the direction of steepest increase.

Take a function of several variables, compute the partial derivative in each direction, and stack them into a vector. That vector — the gradient, ∇f — has a beautiful property: its direction is the steepest uphill direction, and its length is how steep that steepest direction is.

Nothing new was invented here: it's Week 1's partials, reorganized into a single object. That reorganization is what makes it powerful — one arrow summarizes how a whole landscape rises.

Why it matters: gradient descent (following −∇f downhill) is how essentially every modern machine-learning model is trained, and in physics, force literally is a negative gradient (Block 3).
1.4MathematicsW3 · D2Directional DerivativesHow fast does the function change if I walk in this particular direction?

The gradient tells you the steepest direction — but the direction you care about (a specific path, a specific intervention) is rarely the steepest one. The directional derivative answers the general question, and the formula is clean: dot the gradient with your chosen direction vector.

Two immediate consequences: the change is largest along the gradient itself, and zero perpendicular to it (walking along a contour line — no climb).

1.5MathematicsW2 · D4Optimization — Why Zero Derivative Means Max or MinAt a peak the curve is momentarily flat — and "momentarily flat" is exactly what a zero derivative means.

A function's maximum or minimum is where it stops rising and starts falling (or vice versa). At that turning point the tangent is horizontal, so f′ = 0. That's the entire logic — no algebra tricks required. One honesty note: not every zero-derivative point is a max or min (some are inflection points), but the core "flat = extremum candidate" idea is the payload.

Why it matters: this is the machinery under every later "the system settles at minimum energy" claim — protein folding, least action, free energy — and under fitting models to data.
1.6MathematicsW1 · D4Local Approximation & Taylor SeriesNear any point, a curve is almost its tangent line; add curvature terms and the approximation keeps improving.

Very close to a point, a smooth curve and its tangent line are nearly indistinguishable — that's local linear approximation, and the tangent is the best possible straight-line fit there. A Taylor series extends the idea: add a term for curvature, then one for how curvature changes, and so on. More terms = better local approximation.

This is literally how calculators compute sin(x) and eˣ — polynomials are easy to evaluate; transcendental functions aren't.

Why it matters: physicists linearize systems near equilibrium (that's where Block 3's oscillators come from), and simple first-order models in pharmacology are exactly this "start with the linear approximation" habit.
1.7MathematicsW1 · D3Logarithms & Log ScalesA log answers "ten to what power gives this number?" — so equal steps on a log scale mean equal multiplications.

log(1000) = 3 because 10³ = 1000. On a log scale, moving one step multiplies by ten: pH 4 → pH 5 is a ten-fold change in acidity, not a small nudge. That's why pH, decibels, drug potency, and bacterial counts all live on log axes — the quantities span many orders of magnitude and only become readable there.

Two bonus facts to keep: adding on a log scale = multiplying on a linear one, and an exponential curve becomes a straight line on a log axis — the visual signature you'll use to spot exponential decay in Block 2.

Day 1 · Block 2

Accumulation & the Equations of Life

~40 min · Integration → the Fundamental Theorem → differential equations → exponential & logistic growth → half-life → where the tidy model breaks.

2.1MathematicsW2 · D1Integration as AccumulationGiven a rate, how much has piled up in total? That's integration — area under the curve is just the picture of it.

Distance is the accumulation of speed over time. The trick behind integration: slice the problem into infinitely thin rectangles and add them up — precision comes from the slices getting thinner, not from a clever shortcut. Hold onto the word accumulation rather than "area": area is only the visual that appears when you graph rate against time.

It's the mirror image of the derivative: a derivative zooms in for a local rate; an integral zooms out for a total.

Why it matters: total drug exposure over time — the pharmacological AUC (area under the curve) used directly in dosing decisions — is literally this integral applied to a concentration–time graph.
2.2MathematicsW2 · D2The Fundamental Theorem of CalculusDifferentiation and integration are inverse operations — one undoes the other.

Concretely: the rate at which "accumulated area so far" grows is just the original function's value at that point. Two ideas that look unrelated — instant rate and total accumulation — turn out to be mirror images. Historically this connecting insight, not derivatives or integrals alone, is why Newton and Leibniz are credited as co-inventors of calculus: it made calculus one usable tool instead of two clever tricks.

Why it matters: the FTC is your formal license to shuttle between "I have a rate" and "I want a total" — which you'll do constantly with drug curves, growth curves, and reaction rates.
2.3PhysicsW2 · D6Introduction to Differential EquationsAn equation that relates a quantity to its own rate of change — and you can often reason about it without solving it.

dN/dt = rN says: "the population's growth rate is proportional to the current population." That's a differential equation. Flip the sign — dN/dt = −λN — and you have radioactive decay. Growth and decay are the same equation, opposite direction.

The deeper lesson: many real biological equations have no clean closed-form solution, so the more transferable skill is extracting qualitative behaviour straight from the equation's structure — does this grow, decay, oscillate, or stabilize? (Vector fields and phase space are the visual tools for that.)

2.4MathematicsW3 · D3Growth & Logistic Models, ProperlyExponential growth runs unchecked; the logistic equation adds a brake that engages as the population nears its ceiling.
dN/dt = rN  →  N(t) = N₀e^{rt}

Unconstrained exponential growth — up forever. Real growth hits limits, so we add one factor:

dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K)

The (1 − N/K) term is the whole story. When N is small compared to the carrying capacity K, it's ≈1 and growth looks exponential. As N approaches K, it shrinks toward 0 and growth stalls — the curve bends into an S-shape and flattens. Because you can see why each parameter matters, you now own a model you can adapt (new K, new r), not a formula you can only recognize.

Why it matters: the logistic model is the standard first model for bacterial cultures, early epidemics, and some tumor-growth models — most real growth eventually hits a resource constraint.
2.5BiologyW2 · D2Modelling Decay Across a MembraneConcentration falling across a membrane traces an exponential-decay curve — the same shape as radioactive decay and drug clearance.

This was a flag-planting day, and the flag says: exponential decay means the rate of decrease is proportional to how much is currently present — more molecule, faster decline; less, slower. Once you notice that membrane diffusion, radioactive decay, and drug elimination all produce the identical curve, you stop memorizing three facts and start recognizing one pattern in three fields. "Half-life" — the time for half of whatever's left to disappear — is a property of exponential decay specifically, not of decline in general.

2.6MathematicsW4 · D1Solving Exponential Decay & Half-LifeSolve dN/dt = −λN and a gem falls out: half-life doesn't depend on how much you started with.
N(t) = N₀e^{−λt}  ·  t½ = ln2 / λ

Why is half-life dose-independent? Because exponential decay's defining property is that a constant proportion — not a constant amount — is lost per unit time. Half of 800 mg goes in the same time as half of 8 mg. Derive it once by hand and it becomes obvious rather than magical.

Why it matters: this is why a doctor can state "this drug has a 6-hour half-life" as one dose-independent fact. Half-life is required in official drug labeling and is a core USMLE-tested dosing concept.
2.7MathematicsW4 · D2Multi-Compartment ThinkingReal drugs absorb, then distribute, then eliminate — so real curves have two phases, and the tidy single-exponential model is only the first draft.

Single-compartment thinking treats the body as one well-mixed pool. Reality: a drug is absorbed, then distributed into tissues, then eliminated — three processes, each with its own rate. The result is often a two-phase concentration curve: a sharp early drop (distribution) followed by a slower decline (true elimination).

This was a deliberately humbling day: knowing where the simple model breaks down is itself real understanding, and healthy skepticism toward the simplest model is a scientific habit, not a defeat.

Day 1 · Block 3

The Physics of Why

~35 min · Least action → Noether's symmetry → force as a gradient → oscillators → entropy. The "why does nature settle where it settles" block.

3.1PhysicsW1 · D3The Principle of Least ActionNewton's laws describe motion instant by instant; least action describes the whole path at once — nature "picks" the path that makes action stationary.

Action = the integral of (kinetic energy − potential energy) over the whole path; that quantity inside is called the Lagrangian. Instead of local, moment-by-moment rules, the least-action view is a global optimization: among all conceivable paths, the real one extremizes action. (Pedantic but true: formally it's stationary action, not always a strict minimum.)

It took two centuries — Maupertuis → Euler → Lagrange — to go from ridiculed guess to rigorous principle. The perspective shift itself, local rule → global optimization, is the takeaway; you meet it again anywhere something is found by minimizing a cost.

Why it matters: this one principle quietly underlies classical mechanics, optics (Fermat's principle), and — as Feynman's path integral — quantum mechanics itself.
3.2PhysicsW1 · D6Symmetry & Conservation Laws (Noether's Theorem)Every continuous symmetry corresponds to a conserved quantity — conservation laws are derived, not just observed.

Emmy Noether, 1918: if the laws of physics don't change when you shift something continuously, a quantity must be conserved. Time-translation symmetry (physics works the same today as tomorrow) → conservation of energy. Space-translation symmetry (same here as there) → conservation of momentum.

The direction of the logic is the profound part: physicists didn't observe energy conservation and hunt for a reason — Noether showed conservation is a mathematical consequence of symmetry. Know a system's symmetries and you can derive what must be conserved with no new experiment. Her proof is built on the Lagrangian from the least-action day.

3.3PhysicsW2 · D3Symmetry & Conservation, One Level DeeperRotation → angular momentum; abstract gauge symmetries → conservation of electric charge. This recipe built the Standard Model.

Beyond the big two: rotational symmetry (rotate the whole system, physics unchanged) gives conservation of angular momentum, and more abstract gauge symmetries explain conservation of electric charge. The sharpening question from this rewatch day: if a law of physics looked different on a Tuesday than a Wednesday, energy conservation would break — pushing on why that hypothetical forces the conclusion is the real exercise.

Particle physicists literally build theories this way: propose a symmetry, derive its conserved quantity, test against experiment — the cumulative method behind the Standard Model, which predicted the Higgs boson decades before its 2012 discovery.

3.4PhysicsW3 · D1Force as Negative Gradient of Potential EnergyF = −∇U: force always points toward decreasing potential energy — a ball rolls in the direction of steepest descent.
F = −∇U

Yesterday's math gradient doing real explanatory work: instead of "force" and "potential energy" being two memorized concepts, one completely determines the other. The negative sign is the downhill arrow.

The implication worth sitting with: equilibrium isn't a separate concept from energy minimization. "Force is zero" and "gradient of potential energy is zero" are mathematically the same statement — connecting straight back to zero-derivative optimization.

3.5PhysicsW3 · D5OscillatorsSystems near a stable equilibrium tend to swing around it — and knowing where the analogy breaks is the mature skill.

A pendulum or a mass on a spring doesn't just sit at equilibrium — it oscillates around it. The equation of motion is itself a differential equation, and mathematically the oscillation emerges from linearizing the system near equilibrium — Week 1's local-approximation idea returning with a job.

The caution built into that day: a spring, a pendulum, and a predator–prey cycle all "oscillate," but they are different equations that merely share a qualitative shape. Collecting more things that wobble is easy; finding where the analogy breaks (different driving mechanisms, different math) is the scientist's habit.

Why it matters: a heartbeat is structurally an oscillating system (with its own mechanism), and stability-near-equilibrium math recurs across physiology, ecology, and engineering.
3.6PhysicsW4 · D3Entropy & Free EnergyEntropy counts the ways a state can exist; "the egg won't unscramble" is a statement about probability, not a separate law.

Better than "disorder": entropy is the number of microscopic arrangements (microstates) consistent with what you see. There are vastly more scrambled-egg arrangements than whole-egg arrangements, so the second law — an isolated system's entropy tends to increase — is overwhelming probability, not decree. It's why heat flows one way and perpetual-motion machines fail.

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

Free energy balances energy against entropy. A protein can fold into a more ordered, lower-entropy shape when the energy saving outweighs the entropy cost — this is the formal payoff of the folding-funnel and least-action thread that started in Week 1. A tiny mutation can destabilize a fold by shifting that balance just enough to favour the unfolded, higher-entropy state.

Fact break — 6 quick ones from the plan's fact deck
  • Noether's theorem says every continuous symmetry of a system has a matching conserved quantity — symmetry and conservation are one fact seen twice.
  • The Higgs boson was found in 2012 — confirming, nearly 50 years later, a particle first predicted on paper in 1964.
  • Almost every atom heavier than hydrogen in your body was forged inside a star. "We are made of star-stuff" is meant literally.
  • A teaspoon of neutron-star material would weigh about a billion tonnes — matter so dense that atoms themselves are crushed.
  • A lightning bolt heats the air around it to about 30,000 C — roughly five times hotter than the Sun's surface.
  • Strip the empty space out of its atoms and the entire human race would fit inside a single sugar cube.
Day 1 · Block 4

The Cell as a Rate Machine

~50 min · Membranes → signalling → folding → enzymes → the Nernst battery → respiration → pH, buffers & acid–base. The densest block — take it slower.

4.1BiologyW1 · D1Cell Membranes & TransportThe membrane isn't a wall — it's a selective filter, and selectivity is what makes a cell a cell.

The fluid mosaic membrane is flexible, not rigid. Passive transport (diffusion, facilitated diffusion) costs no cellular energy; active transport (pumps like Na⁺/K⁺) costs energy because it moves things against their gradient. And Fick's law — flux is proportional to the concentration gradient — is Day 1's derivative applied to a real biological rate.

The deepest point: a cell that let everything in and out freely couldn't maintain the internal chemistry that makes it alive. Selectivity, not openness, is the point.

Why it matters: membrane-transport failures underlie a huge clinical range — cystic fibrosis is a chloride-channel defect, and most diuretics work by targeting transporters.
4.2BiologyW1 · D2Cell SignallingReception → transduction → response — and amplification is why a nanomolar hormone can move the whole body.

Three stages: a receptor binds a ligand (reception), the signal is relayed and amplified inside the cell (transduction), and the cell does something (response). The high-payoff idea is amplification: one ligand molecule can trigger a cascade activating thousands of downstream molecules — which is exactly why hormones work at vanishingly small concentrations, and why a small dose of a potent drug works at all.

Receptors are multivariable systems too: response depends on ligand concentration, receptor density, and time together — Week 1's partial-derivative idea in the flesh.

Why it matters: most modern drugs — beta-blockers, SSRIs, insulin, most cancer therapies — act by binding a receptor and altering a signalling cascade, not by brute force.
4.3BiologyW1 · D5Protein Structure & FoldingShape is function — and folding is a fast slide down a funnel-shaped energy landscape, not a search of every possibility.

Four levels: primary (amino-acid sequence) → secondary (local patterns like α-helices) → tertiary (full 3D shape) → quaternary (multiple chains). Folding is driven mainly by the hydrophobic effect — water-avoiding side chains bury themselves inside, away from the watery cytoplasm.

The gold: searching every possible 3D shape would take longer than the age of the universe (Levinthal's paradox), yet proteins fold in milliseconds — because the energy landscape is a funnel guiding a fast slide toward the free-energy minimum. Notice it rhymes with least action: another system settling at an extremum. An enzyme's active site is a precise 3D pocket; fold even slightly wrong and function is usually lost entirely.

Why it matters: Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, and prion diseases are, mechanistically, misfolding disorders — a core reason structural biology is so heavily funded.
4.4BiologyW2 · D1Enzyme Kinetics & Michaelis–MentenRate rises with substrate, then plateaus — because the reaction becomes enzyme-limited, not substrate-limited.

Plot reaction rate against substrate concentration and you get a rectangular hyperbola. Vmax = the ceiling, reached once every enzyme active site is saturated. Km = the substrate concentration at exactly half Vmax — a practical measure of how "eager" the enzyme is for its substrate (lower Km = higher affinity).

Why the plateau exists is the actual concept: once every active site is occupied, adding more substrate can't speed anything up. (Lineweaver–Burk straight-line plots are exam furniture — learn to read one, but they're not the idea.)

Why it matters: liver drug-metabolism enzymes follow this model; saturable clearance is what makes ethanol and phenytoin disproportionately dangerous in overdose — their enzymes max out at clinically relevant concentrations.
4.5BiologyW2 · D3Membrane Potentials & the Nernst EquationPumps make ion gradients; a logarithm turns the concentration ratio into a voltage — the resting cell is a tiny battery at about −70 mV.

Pumps concentrate K⁺ inside and Na⁺ outside, separating charge across the membrane. The Nernst equation converts an ion's concentration ratio into a voltage — via a logarithm (Week 1's log day, now electrified). Open the right channels and ions rush down their gradients; the voltage briefly flips — that spike is the action potential behind every nerve and muscle signal.

NCERT says a nerve impulse happens; this tells you why, as a number you can compute.

Why it matters: change the K⁺ gradient and you change the resting potential of every excitable cell — including the heart. That's why blood potassium is watched so closely: severe hyperkalaemia can stop the heart by collapsing this voltage.
4.6BiologyW2 · D5Cell Respiration & MetabolismEnergy is released in small, controlled steps — and the slowest step controls the speed of the whole pathway.

Three major stages: glycolysis (cytoplasm) → Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix) → electron transport chain (inner mitochondrial membrane). Why steps at all? Releasing all of glucose's energy in one reaction would be an uncontrolled burst of heat, incompatible with a cell's narrow temperature tolerance.

The transferable idea over the intermediate names: the rate-limiting step. A chain is only as fast as its slowest link — the same logic as enzyme kinetics, drug metabolism, and any multi-step process you'll ever analyse. Find the slowest step and you've found what controls the system.

4.7BiologyW2 · D5pH, Acids, Bases & BufferspH is a log scale of hydrogen ions, and a buffer doesn't stop pH change — it blunts it.

pH = −log[H⁺], so pH 6 is ten times more acidic than pH 7 — Week 1's log-scale rule, applied. A buffer is a mixture that resists pH change when acid or base is added; blood's main one is the bicarbonate system.

Human blood is defended inside a razor-thin window: about 7.35–7.45. Small deviations — acidosis, alkalosis — are medical emergencies. The pairing with respiration is deliberate: the CO₂ your metabolism produces becomes carbonic acid, which the buffer manages and the lungs breathe off.

4.8BiologyW2 · D5Acid–Base Balance in the BodypH tracks the ratio of bicarbonate to CO₂ — lungs move CO₂ fast, kidneys move bicarbonate slowly, and each compensates for the other.

Three defenders of blood pH ≈ 7.40, on three timescales: the bicarbonate buffer (instant), the lungs blowing off CO₂ (minutes), the kidneys adjusting bicarbonate (hours–days). Henderson–Hasselbalch says pH tracks the ratio HCO₃⁻ : dissolved CO₂ (normally about 20:1) — a log relationship, not either number alone.

Too much CO₂ (a lung problem) → respiratory acidosis; too little bicarbonate (a metabolic problem, e.g. diabetic ketoacidosis) → metabolic acidosis. Compensation = the body adjusting the other component to defend the ratio, like breathing faster to blow off CO₂ when acid accumulates. Get this story and blood-gas interpretation later becomes bookkeeping.

Why it matters: arterial blood gases are read on ward rounds every single day — this is among the highest-yield physiology in clinical medicine, and NCERT gives only the chemistry, never the physiology.
Fact break — 6 quick ones from the plan's fact deck
  • Some enzymes speed reactions by a factor near 10^17 — turning a process that would take millions of years into one that finishes in milliseconds.
  • Uncoiled, the DNA in one human cell stretches about 2 metres; across all your cells it would reach the Sun and back many times over.
  • Your stomach lining renews itself every few days — without it, the acid would begin to digest your own stomach.
  • Your blood is held between roughly pH 7.35 and 7.45; drifting outside that narrow band is a medical emergency, which is why the body defends it so hard.
  • Only about 1 to 2 percent of the human genome codes for proteins; much of the rest regulates when and how those genes switch on.
  • Photosynthesis and your mitochondria run nearly the same chemistry in reverse — one banks solar energy, the other spends it.

Populations, Drugs & Outbreaks

~45 min · Hardy–Weinberg → real growth → feedback loops → pharmacokinetics → dose–response → R₀ → the grand epidemic synthesis.

5.1BiologyW3 · D2Hardy–Weinberg EquilibriumA null model: what allele frequencies would do if nothing were acting — so deviation from it is itself evidence.
p² + 2pq + q² = 1

Five conditions for equilibrium: no mutation, random mating, no gene flow, infinite population size, no selection. Real populations almost always violate at least one — and that's the point. Hardy–Weinberg describes the baseline of "nothing changing," so when a real population's genotype frequencies deviate, the deviation is evidence that some evolutionary force is acting.

The reusable reasoning tool: build a no-change baseline, then look for deviation. In real genetics research, HWE testing is a standard quality-control step — deviation in a control group can signal genotyping errors before it signals biology.

5.2BiologyW3 · D4Population Growth in Real OrganismsBacteria in fresh culture trace a near-textbook logistic curve — but real populations rarely follow the ideal S perfectly.

Yesterday's abstract (1 − N/K) becomes observable: bacterial growth is exponential at first, then flattens as nutrients deplete and waste accumulates. But real ecology complicates the clean S-curve — overshoot-and-crash dynamics, predator–prey cycles, seasonality.

And "carrying capacity" is scale-dependent: for a bacterial colony it's nutrients and space; for humans it's a genuinely contested, multi-factor open question — technology has repeatedly raised what looked like hard limits. Sitting with that ambiguity is more honest than resolving it with a Google search.

5.3BiologyW3 · D5Feedback Loops & HomeostasisNegative feedback means stability; positive feedback means escalation. Classify the loop and you understand most of what it does.

Homeostasis is the body holding key variables — temperature, blood glucose, pH — in a narrow range despite a changing environment. Negative feedback counteracts a change to return to a set point: the workhorse of physiology (thermoregulation, glucose control). Positive feedback amplifies a change — rarer, reserved for fast decisive events: childbirth contractions, blood clotting.

Every loop shares one architecture: a sensor, a control centre, and an effector — a thermostat, in flesh.

Why it matters: nearly every MBBS system — endocrine, renal, cardiovascular, respiratory — is a set of feedback loops defending a set point; disease is very often a loop broken or hijacked.
5.4BiologyW4 · D1PharmacokineticsBlood drug concentration falls along the exponential-decay curve — first-order elimination — and half-life is the number doctors dose by.

The topic the whole month built toward. Most drugs show first-order elimination kinetics: concentration falls exponentially, so a constant proportion is cleared per unit time — the same equation you solved in Block 2. Half-life, not the raw rate constant, is what clinicians actually use to set dosing intervals; and a drug with a 6-hour half-life might still be prescribed every 8 hours, because the interval also weighs the efficacy window, side-effects, and patient adherence.

Why it matters: renal impairment slows the elimination of many drugs, and that is identified in clinical literature as a leading cause of preventable adverse drug reactions — this math is the literal mechanism behind real patient harm and its prevention.
5.5BiologyW4 · D2Dose–Response CurvesAn S-shaped curve with a plateau — the same saturation story as Michaelis–Menten, relabelled for pharmacology.

Most drugs follow a sigmoidal dose–response curve: effect rises slowly, then steeply, then plateaus. EC50 — the dose producing half the maximum effect — is the pharmacodynamic cousin of enzyme kinetics' Km. The plateau exists for the same reason as enzyme saturation: once all relevant receptors are occupied, more drug can't produce more effect.

The classic trap to never fall into: pharmacokinetics asks how concentration changes over time; pharmacodynamics asks what effect a given concentration produces. Two different questions that combine to determine a drug's real behaviour — conflating them is a documented source of reasoning errors in early clinical training.

5.6BiologyW4 · D5Epidemiology Basics & R₀R₀ = average new infections from one case in a fully susceptible population. Above 1 it grows, below 1 it dies — and it is one of public health's most misused numbers.

First the vocabulary: incidence = new cases over a period; prevalence = total existing cases at a moment. Then the famous number: R₀ > 1 means an outbreak tends to grow; R₀ < 1 means it dies out — the same sign logic as dN/dt.

Three corrections that put you ahead of most reporting (per the CDC's own literature): R₀ is not disease severity, not a fixed biological constant (it depends on social behaviour and setting), and not directly lowered by vaccination — vaccines change the effective reproduction number Rt, which reflects real-world immunity and interventions.

5.7BiologyW5 · D2Logistic Growth Meets EpidemiologyEarly outbreaks look exponential because the logistic brake hasn't engaged; the S-bend comes when susceptibles run out.

Pure synthesis, no new content: in an epidemic, K is roughly the susceptible population. Early on, N is tiny against K, so (1 − N/K) ≈ 1 and growth looks deceptively exponential. The bend into the S-curve happens precisely as the susceptible pool depletes.

Communication tip that doubles as intuition: doubling time (how long until cases double) is a more intuitive statement of exponential growth than the raw rate r — and it's the number that should trigger early concern, before the curve visibly bends. This one picture fuses Week 3's logistic equation, Week 4's R₀, and Bayes-style reasoning into how outbreaks are actually modelled.

Thinking Like a Scientist

~50 min · The statistics of evidence (normal curve → p-values → Bayes → test characteristics) and the full research-literacy ladder, from your first paper to finding your own.

6.1MathematicsW4 · D3The Normal Distribution & Standard DeviationTwo numbers fix the whole bell: mean sets the centre, standard deviation sets the width — and 95% lives within two SDs.

The normal curve is completely described by its mean and its standard deviation (typical distance from the mean — the natural unit for "how unusual is this value?"). The 68–95–99.7 rule: ~68% of values fall within 1 SD, ~95% within 2 SD, ~99.7% within 3 SD.

That "95% within two standard deviations" is exactly where p < 0.05 and the 95% confidence interval come from — which is why this was scheduled one day before p-values: it turns the next topic from memorisation into understanding.

Why it matters: reference ranges for lab values, growth charts, and the whole machinery of significance testing assume or approximate this curve.
6.2MathematicsW4 · D4p-values, Confidence Intervals & Statistical SignificanceA p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme IF there were no real effect — it is NOT the probability the null hypothesis is true.

Say the precise version until it's reflex: p = P(data this extreme | null hypothesis true). The popular reading — "5% chance the null is true" — is wrong, and surveys of working researchers show this misinterpretation is widespread, feeding the replication crisis.

Two more guards: "statistically significant" ≠ "clinically important" (p < 0.05 only says the data would be surprising under no-effect), and a confidence interval gives the range of plausible true effects — a wide interval signals real uncertainty, even around an impressive-looking average.

Why it matters: you'll be a consumer of trial statistics as a physician and a producer of them as a researcher — getting the actual meaning right is one of the highest-leverage corrections in the whole plan.
6.3MathematicsW5 · D1Bayesian Thinking & Bayes' TheoremNew evidence should update your prior belief, not replace it — and ignoring the base rate is how a positive test fools even doctors.

The classic illustration: a disease affects 1% of people; a test is 90% accurate. Someone tests positive — do they probably have it? Run natural frequencies: of 1,000 people, ~10 have the disease and ~9 test true-positive; of the ~990 healthy, ~99 test false-positive. So a positive result is roughly 9 true vs 99 false — far more likely false than true.

Ignoring how rare the condition is = base-rate neglect, and studies since Gigerenzer & Hoffrage (1995) show practicing physicians make this error too — estimating ~90% when the true answer is single digits. The fix is genuinely actionable: reframe percentages as natural frequencies ("X out of 1,000 people") and correct reasoning improves dramatically.

Why it matters: arguably the single highest-stakes topic in the plan for a clinician — it's the formal structure of every "what does this test result actually mean for this patient?" judgment.
6.4ResearchW5 · D1Sensitivity, Specificity & Diagnostic TestsSensitivity and specificity belong to the test; what a positive result means for a patient depends on prevalence.

Sensitivity = of people who truly have the disease, the fraction the test catches (few false negatives). Specificity = of the truly healthy, the fraction correctly cleared (few false positives). Memory hook: SnNout — a Sensitive test, Negative result, rules out; SpPin — a Specific test, Positive result, rules in.

The clinical twin of Bayes: the test's numbers are fixed properties, but the chance a positive is truly positive swings entirely on how common the disease is — the base-rate idea, stated in the language of the ward.

6.5ResearchW2 · D4Correlation vs CausationWhen A and B move together, there are always four options — and a hidden third cause is the usual culprit.

The move to internalise: before concluding anything from "A is linked to B," list the possibilities — A causes B, B causes A, a hidden C causes both (a confounder), or coincidence. Keep one question loaded for every health headline: what third thing might be causing both? It's the right answer far more often than either direct explanation.

Only a controlled, randomised experiment reliably separates causation from correlation — which is the entire reason clinical trials exist. Doctors who skip this step get fooled by observational studies constantly.

6.6ResearchW2 · D4Study Designs & Levels of EvidenceTwo questions place almost any study on the evidence pyramid: did they intervene or observe, and did they look forward or backward?

Studies split into observational (you watch) and experimental (you intervene) — the randomised controlled trial is the experimental gold standard. Among observational designs: case-control looks backward from an outcome, cohort follows groups forward in time, cross-sectional is a snapshot.

The evidence pyramid: systematic reviews & RCTs on top → cohort → case-control → case series → expert opinion. Higher usually means better protection against bias and confounding — but execution matters as much as design: a sloppy RCT can be worse than a careful cohort study.

Why it matters: "a case-control study found a link" is weaker than "an RCT showed an effect" — knowing the menu is the single most useful filter for reading medical literature, and it's how doctors weigh studies that disagree.
6.7ResearchW2 · D4Identifying Experimental DesignFind the variables, the control group, the sample size — and ask the hardest question: what would have proven them wrong?

Reading with a design lens means extracting four things: the independent variable (what researchers changed), the dependent variable (what they measured), the control group (without one, you can't tell intervention from coincidence), and the sample size (a result from 8 participants weighs very differently than from 800).

The falsifiability question — what data would have counted as evidence AGAINST the hypothesis? — is the hardest and most valuable in the whole reading thread. A paper that can't fail a test isn't really testing anything, and abstracts actively obscure this.

6.8ResearchW1 · D4Reading Your First PaperRead the introduction before the abstract, chase two questions only, and treat "understood half of it" as a pass.

The abstract is written last and optimized to sell — read the introduction first so the authors' framing doesn't bias you. Chase exactly two questions: what question is this paper trying to answer, and why does it matter? Write down every unfamiliar term and look it up — scientific vocabulary is precise on purpose, and skipping one word usually costs you the next three sentences.

The real skill being built wasn't comprehension of that one paper — it was tolerance for productive confusion, which determines whether someone keeps reading primary literature after medical school starts or quietly stops. (Your first assigned paper: Watson & Crick's one-page 1953 DNA-structure letter.)

6.9ResearchW1 · D6Reading an Original Research PaperOriginal research reports new data — find the hypothesis, then check the gap between what was measured and what was claimed.

Original papers differ from reviews: they report new data, so Methods and Results carry the real weight. Before reading the results, identify the hypothesis — usually buried at the end of the introduction, rarely a clean sentence — and ask what outcome would have proven the authors wrong.

Then the auditor's move: note exactly what was measured and how — the gap between "what they claim" and "what they actually measured" is where most scientific overreach hides. Keshav's three-pass method (skim → focused hour → deep critical pass) teaches the underlying habit: read as deeply as your purpose needs, not cover-to-cover every time.

6.10ResearchW2 · D6Skimming AbstractsTriage many abstracts fast, hunting for recurring methods and recurring open questions — abstracts sell, so verify later.

Working scientists face hundreds of new papers; skimming abstracts is how they triage which deserve a full read. The pattern-hunt across 3–4 abstracts in one topic: do multiple groups use the same technique? What do authors repeatedly flag as unresolved? That maps "what does this field currently care about" faster than reading any single paper fully.

Standing caution: abstracts are written last and optimized to sell the paper — treat their claims as hypotheses to verify when you read the full text.

6.11ResearchW3 · D4Reading a Population-Data PaperSpot the mathematical tool in the methods section before judging the biology.

The first test of whether this plan's math transfers to real literature: open a genetics, epidemiology, or ecology paper and identify which mathematical or statistical tool it leans on — a growth model? a regression? a significance test? — then ask whether you can roughly explain why that tool fits this kind of data.

Recognizing "oh, this is a logistic-growth-style model" inside an unfamiliar paper's methods is a genuinely harder skill than recognizing the same model in a textbook. (Your assigned example: a 2020 open-access paper fitting logistic and SIR models to Indian COVID data — the exact dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K) you'd just learned, sitting inside a real study.)

6.12ResearchW4 · D4Reading a Clinical TrialFind the pre-specified primary outcome, check the power, and test whether the discussion's prose matches its own statistics.

Three moves on any trial: identify the primary outcome — the single pre-specified measure the trial was designed to detect, distinct from secondary outcomes mentioned afterward; check the sample size and whether it was adequately powered for the effect they sought; then the audit — does the discussion's language match what the reported p-value and confidence interval actually support, or does it quietly oversell?

This is the convergence point of the entire evidence thread. (Your assigned example: the RECOVERY trial of dexamethasone in COVID-19 — a landmark RCT with one clean primary outcome, 28-day mortality.) ClinicalTrials.gov lets you compare a trial's pre-registered design against the published paper.

6.13ResearchW5 · D3Navigating the Literature — PubMed & Google ScholarPlain keywords are enough to start; the real skill is citation chaining — references point backward, "Cited by" points forward.

Don't learn MeSH terms on day one — a plain keyword search is plenty. Then chain: a paper's reference list maps the older, foundational work (backward); Google Scholar's "Cited by" shows the newer work building on it (forward). That's how researchers map a field's actual conversation, not one paper's isolated claim.

This day marked the handoff: every earlier reading day assigned you papers; here you found three yourself on a self-chosen question — the skill that separates doing assigned reading from having a research question and going to find what's known.

6.14ResearchW5 · D4Reading Your Own PaperThe capstone: a paper nobody assigned, read with everything you've built — and where you get stuck is data, not failure.

Same three questions as all month: what are they asking, what did they actually do, what evidence convinced them? The difference is motivational: nobody assigned this paper, so pushing through confusion is entirely self-generated — which is what sustained research engagement actually feels like.

Notice where the month's vocabulary — derivatives, exponential models, p-values, Bayesian reasoning — appears in the paper, even disguised in different notation. And if you hit a wall two pages in, write down exactly where: that's more diagnostic about where you are than finishing something easy would have been. Contrast with Week 1's standard ("understanding half is a pass") — the shift from tolerated confusion to motivated comprehension is the month's real result.

Stage 0

The plain-words picture, the full pipeline, and the vocabulary

A paper is a structured document; a journal is a website with an editorial team; peer review is 2–3 volunteer experts sending comments; "published" means your work has a permanent ID and lives online forever.

In plain words: you write a document in a standard format (called a manuscript). You upload it to a journal's website. An editor glances at it and either bounces it back (a "desk rejection") or sends it to 2–3 unpaid experts (peer reviewers) who write comments. You fix things and reply. Pick your lane — first, what "lane" even means

A "lane" is just this guide's word for the type of study — the shape of the work you'll do every day. Like picking which sport you're playing before you start training: it decides your daily work, your Methods section, your ethics needs, and which journals will take the paper.

There are about seven realistic shapes a first paper can take. Below, each one gets the full treatment: what it is in plain words, what your hands actually do, a real example title, and what the finished paper contains. Read all seven, then use the 60-second chooser at the end.

First, the three "reviews," told apart — this trio confuses everyone. All three mean "a paper made by studying other papers, not patients." The difference is how strict the rules are. A narrative review has no formal rules: you choose papers and tell the story. A scoping review adds rules: you write down your search and your in/out criteria before reading, then report what the whole territory looks like. A systematic review is the strictest: one narrow question, exhaustive multi-database search, usually two independent screeners, and a quality rating of every included study. (If a systematic review also mathematically pools the numbers from all studies into one combined estimate, that pooling is called a meta-analysis.)

Lane 1 · Narrative review possible now

What it is: you read the important papers on one topic and write an organized, cited explanation of what's known — honestly, the world's most rigorous essay.

What you actually do: search (Step 3) → read 20–40 papers into your matrix → group the ideas into 3–5 themes → write the story of the field, theme by theme, citing as you go.

Example title: "Base-rate neglect in medical test interpretation: a narrative review."

You end with: mostly text, one summary table, ~30–60 references.

The catch: with no formal rules on which papers you included, a reviewer can ask "how do I know you didn't cherry-pick?" — which is exactly why the next two lanes exist. Great for learning; middling for a first publication.

Lane 2 · Scoping review possible now — strong first paper

What it is: a review with rules. Before reading anything, you write down exactly how you'll search and what counts as in or out. Then you map a territory: what has been studied, with which methods, and where the holes are. You are not answering "does X work?" — you are charting "what exists?"

What you actually do: define the search string and in/out criteria (Step 4) → run the search → screen every title/abstract against your rules, keeping counts of what you excluded and why → read the included set → chart each paper's characteristics into one big table → describe the map you found.

Example title: "How published studies define and source R₀ for dengue: a scoping review."

You end with: a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram (the counts), one large characteristics table, and text describing patterns and gaps.

Why it works for you: no lab, no committee, finishable alone in ~6 weeks if the question is narrow — and journals respect the rules-first discipline.

Lane 3 · Systematic review possible, but heavy

What it is: the strictest review: one narrow question (often "does X change Y?"), an exhaustive, documented search across multiple databases, ideally two people screening independently, and a formal quality/bias rating of every included study.

What you actually do: everything in Lane 2, plus more databases, plus double screening, plus bias assessment of each study.

Example title: "Effect of natural-frequency formats on diagnostic reasoning accuracy: a systematic review."

You end with: PRISMA flow + evidence tables + (if you pool numbers) a meta-analysis forest plot.

Honest note: gold-standard prestige, but genuinely a team sport — usually 2+ people. A sane path: do the scoping review first, upgrade later with a co-author.

Lane 4 · Bibliometric / meta-research easiest full pipeline — our worked example

What it is: research about research. Instead of asking a question about a disease, you ask a question about the papers themselves and answer it by counting. "What fraction of papers do X?" "How did Y change over five years?"

What you actually do: define a set of papers (one journal, one year, one topic) → open each abstract, free, one by one → record yes/no answers in a spreadsheet, straight from your codebook → compute percentages with confidence intervals → report.

Example title: the worked example — "Reporting of confidence intervals and effect sizes in RCT abstracts of two Indian journals, 2025."

You end with: 1–2 tables, a short IMRaD text, maybe one bar chart.

Why it's the easiest complete pipeline: the data (abstracts) is free to read, the method is counting, and the statistics fit in a spreadsheet. Every skill Block 6 taught is directly the subject matter.

Lane 5 · Secondary data analysis possible now

What it is: answering a new question using data somebody else already collected and openly published. Governments and agencies release giant free tables: NFHS-5 (India's National Family Health Survey — state-by-state health, nutrition, and family data), the WHO Global Health Observatory (world health indicators), Our World in Data (cleaned global datasets), data.gov.in (Indian government data). "Secondary" just means the data's second use — their collection, your question.

What you actually do: pick a dataset → define your variables from its tables → download → descriptive statistics and associations with confidence intervals → interpret cautiously, confounders named out loud (your Block 6 training is the safety rail).

Example title: "State-level association between household sanitation and childhood anaemia: an analysis of NFHS-5 public data."

You end with: tables/figures + a carefully hedged Discussion.

The catch: the temptation to say "causes" when your data can only say "is associated with." Resist it in writing and reviewers will love you.

Lane 6 · Computational / modelling possible now — uses your Python

What it is: build or fit a mathematical model — the plan's own equations — to public data, in code, and report what the model shows. This is exactly what the W3D4 India-COVID paper did with dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K).

What you actually do: get archived case-count or measurement data → write Python that fits the model → compare fitted parameters (growth rate r, ceiling K) across states or waves → plot curves → share the code.

Example title: "Logistic growth parameters of dengue outbreaks across Indian states: a public-data modelling study."

You end with: fitted-curve figures, a parameter table, and a link to your code.

Bonus: framed as hypothesis-driven ("we hypothesised K would differ by state density"), this is the one lane that can also fit JEI's scope while that pre-college window is open (Step 8).

Lane 7 · Survey / cross-sectional later — needs a committee

What it is: you write a questionnaire and collect brand-new answers from real people — a snapshot of a group at one moment.

Why not yet: any study that collects data from people must be approved by an ethics committee before data collection — a panel that checks participants are protected — and journals will refuse the paper without that approval letter. Committees live inside institutions; you'll have one the moment MBBS starts. Park your survey ideas in the question log until then.

Lane 8 · Case report later — needs patients

What it is: a detailed, structured story of one unusual patient — what presented, what was found, what was done, what it teaches.

Why not yet: it needs patient access, the patient's written consent, and institutional sign-off. It's the classic first paper during MBBS clinical years — impossible before.

Step 2

Find the problem — a procedure, not a lightning strike

Mine other papers' Limitations sections, filter through FINER, then shrink the question until it fits in one sentence.

In plain words: you don't invent a question from nothing. Published authors literally announce open problems at the end of their papers — you collect those announcements, pick one, and make it small enough to finish.

Do exactly this (the mining drill, ~90 min):

  1. Pick an area you half-know from the plan: R₀ reporting, base-rate errors, PK dosing, statistics reporting, protein-structure claims.
  2. Open 5 recent papers in it (Step 3 shows how to find them).
  3. From each, copy out only the Limitations paragraph and any "future research should…" sentences into one document.
  4. Cluster the repeats. A gap mentioned by three separate groups is a real, citable gap — and your Introduction is already half-written.

Filter every candidate through FINER:

LetterQuestion to ask
FeasibleCan I finish it in ~6 weeks with my data, skills, and zero budget?
InterestingWould a journal's readers care — not just me?
NovelHas nobody answered exactly this? (Search first — Step 3.)
EthicalDoes it stay in the no-committee lanes above?
RelevantCould someone act differently because of the answer?

Then shrink it. First questions die of scope. Add a time window, a place, a journal set, a disease — until one person can finish it.

Worked example — the question, shrunk three times Too big: "Do medical papers misuse statistics?" → Still big: "How often do trials report confidence intervals?" → Just right: "Among original randomised-trial reports published in 2025 in two Indian medical journals, what proportion report a 95% CI and an effect size for the primary outcome, versus a p-value alone?" — Countable, finishable, and it tests exactly what Block 6 taught.
You now have: one FINER-passing question, written as a single sentence, plus a dated question log for the rejects.
Step 3

Own the literature — search, save, and read into a matrix

A recorded search string + Zotero + a five-column reading matrix turn "I read some papers" into an actual Methods paragraph.

Do exactly this:

  1. Go to pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov. Type plain keywords first. Refine with Boolean operators — just the connector words: AND narrows (both terms must appear), OR widens (either counts), "quotes" lock an exact phrase. Use the left-side filters (year, article type).
  2. Write down your exact final search string and today's date. For any review-type paper, that sentence goes verbatim into your Methods.
  3. Repeat on Google Scholar (broader, less curated).
  4. Chain citations: each good paper's reference list maps the foundations (backward); Scholar's "Cited by" maps the frontier (forward) — your W5D3 skill, now with a purpose.
  5. Install Zotero (zotero.org, free) + its browser connector. One click saves paper, PDF, and citation; later it formats your whole bibliography automatically in any journal's style.
  6. Read with the three-pass method — most papers deserve only pass one — into a matrix, one row per paper, five columns: citation · question asked · design/method · key finding · stated limitation.

Screening hundreds of records for a review? Rayyan (rayyan.ai) — a free website where you click through your search results one by one, marking each include or exclude while it keeps the counts for your PRISMA diagram — has a free tier for exactly this screening stage ✓ verified — optional for small projects, standard for systematic reviews.

Worked example — the searchPubMed: "randomized controlled trial"[Publication Type] AND ("Indian journal" OR [journal names]), filter year 2025 → export the results as a .ris file (a tiny text file that carries citation details between programs — every database can produce one, and Zotero can read it) → import into Zotero → 15-row matrix of similar meta-research papers to learn the standard methods from (they exist — that's good: it proves the method is publishable, and your novelty is the 2025 Indian-journal window).
You now have: a dated search string, a Zotero library, and a ≥15-row reading matrix.
Step 4

Design before you collect — write the rules while you can't cheat yet

A one-page protocol note — criteria, variables, analysis — decided in advance is what makes your result believable, including to you.

In plain words: decide now, before seeing any data, exactly what counts, what you'll measure, and how you'll analyse it. Then the data can't quietly bend you toward the answer you wanted.

Your one-page protocol note contains:

  1. Question (the Step-2 sentence, verbatim).
  2. Inclusion / exclusion criteria — what's in, what's out, with reasons (years, languages, article types, topic bounds).
  3. Variables + codebook — a codebook is simply the dictionary of your spreadsheet: every column's name, its meaning, and its allowed values (e.g., "reports_CI: y/n"), so future-you records data exactly like today-you.
  4. Analysis plan — descriptive statistics first, then effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals. Plain words: an effect size is the size of the finding in real units — "12 percentage points more," "2.3 times the odds" — while a p-value only says whether the data would be surprising if there were no effect. A 95% CI is the range where the true value plausibly lies (Block 6). Report sizes and ranges first, p-values second, and name your tool (spreadsheet / Python).
  5. The falsifiability sentence: "My conclusion would be wrong if the data shows ___." Can't fill the blank? You have an opinion, not a study.

Review lanes: also sketch the PRISMA flow — just a diagram of counts: records found → duplicates removed → screened → excluded (with reasons) → included. (PRISMA is the standard reporting checklist for review papers — the flow diagram of counts is its most famous part; the scoping-review version is called PRISMA-ScR.) That one diagram is the spine of a review paper.

Rigor bonus — pre-registration: post the protocol note publicly on OSF (osf.io), free. "Pre-registration" means putting your plan on record, with a timestamp, before doing the study — so nobody, including you, can quietly move the goalposts later. It signals seriousness far beyond your career stage.

Worked example — the protocol in miniatureInclude: original RCT reports, 2025, journals X & Y. Exclude: protocols, secondary analyses, letters. Codebook per abstract: journal · month · reports p-value for primary outcome (y/n) · reports 95% CI (y/n) · reports effect size (y/n). Analysis: proportions with 95% CIs, overall and per journal. Falsifiability: "I expect <50% report CIs; I'm wrong if ≥80% do — either result is publishable."
You now have: a one-page protocol note (optionally timestamped on OSF). This becomes your Methods section almost word-for-word.

Step 5

Do the work honestly — the audit trail is your credibility

Dated files, a deviations log, and uncertainty reported straight. Deviations noted openly are strengths; hidden ones are misconduct.

Do exactly this:

  1. Make one folder: 01_protocol · 02_search · 03_data · 04_analysis · 05_manuscript.
  2. Extract into a dated sheet, one row per paper or data point, columns straight from your codebook. Nothing extra, nothing "eyeballed."
  3. Version everything: analysis_v3_2026-09-02 beats final_FINAL(2).
  4. When you deviate from the protocol (you will), write what changed and why in a deviations log — it goes transparently into Methods or Limitations.
  5. Compute exactly the planned analysis. Report effect sizes with 95% CIs; describe uncertainty honestly.

Resist making the result "cleaner" than it is: a modest, solid finding publishes; a shiny, shaky one collapses in review. A null or messy result from a sound method is legitimate science — say so plainly.

Worked example — mid-study realityJournal Y's site lists some 2025 issues only as PDFs → deviation log: "Screened Y from print-issue PDFs; 3 issues inaccessible, listed in Limitations." Nobody is upset by this sentence. Everybody is upset by its absence.
You now have: a completed extraction sheet, an analysis file, numbers with CIs, and a deviations log.

Step 7

Ethics & integrity — the five-minute checklist that protects you

Know when a committee is needed, what authorship legally means, and disclose everything a journal asks about.

  1. Ethics approval: studies on people (surveys included) need an Institutional Ethics Committee — which is exactly why your first-paper lanes avoid them. Published papers and public anonymised datasets generally don't require approval, but journals ask you to state this: "Ethics approval was not required as this study analysed only published literature / publicly available anonymised data." Verify the target journal's policy and the dataset's terms.
  2. Authorship (ICMJE — the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, whose rules nearly all medical journals adopt; all four required): substantial contribution · drafting or critical revision · approval of the final version · accountability for the work. Adding someone senior "for weight" (gift authorship) violates this. A mentor who genuinely contributes is an author; one who gave advice goes in Acknowledgements. (Note: some student journals additionally require a senior researcher as co-author — Step 8's table.)
  3. Plagiarism: includes close paraphrase and your own earlier text; journals run similarity software. Cite the source of every idea.
  4. AI disclosure: most journals now have a policy; AI tools can't be authors. Sample sentence: "The authors used [tool] to assist with language editing; all content was reviewed, verified, and approved by the authors, who take full responsibility for the work."
  5. Data availability: "The full data extraction sheet is available as Supplementary Material / at [OSF link]." For a first paper, sharing everything is both easy and impressive.
  6. ORCID: orcid.org → sign up → done. Five minutes, free, yours for life.
You now have: ready-to-paste declaration sentences and an ORCID iD.
Step 8

Publish — preprint, target, submit, survive review

Four sub-moves: make it public (preprint), choose three journals, build the submission package, then answer reviewers like the auditor you've trained to be.

8.1 — Preprint first (public + citable in days)

medRxiv (health/medicine) and bioRxiv (basic biology) are the standard servers; OSF Preprints takes broader work. Verified mechanics ✓ Aug 2026: free; you upload a Word file or PDF plus a funding statement (one sentence saying who paid for the work — yours will read "No funding was received"); screeners check it (days, not months); it gets a DOI, is citable — and is permanent (versions can be updated, but a posted preprint cannot be deleted, so post the version you're proud of). Major journals overwhelmingly allow prior preprints; you simply disclose the preprint DOI in your cover letter. medRxiv can even transmit your files directly to many journals, saving re-entry. One rule: pick one server — cross-posting isn't allowed.

  2. Cross-check candidates on DOAJ (doaj.org) and use its "Without article processing charges" filter ✓ verified — roughly two-thirds of DOAJ-listed journals charge authors nothing ("diamond OA").
  3. Rank a shortlist of three: dream, realistic, safe. You'll work down the list.
